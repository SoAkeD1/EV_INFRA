1. Executive summary

A charger map, a gap heat-map and a forecast all answer the question "what exists?". The Ministry of Heavy Industries (MHI), ICAT and state agencies face a harder one: "Where should the next rupee of public money go, and how will we know it worked?"

We are building a Charging Infrastructure Decision System. The national GIS map is one view of it. The system does four jobs that officials actually have to do:

  1. Trust the data. Reconcile conflicting official counts (27,737 vs 52,718 stations nationally; 2,400 / 5,765 / 6,805 for Karnataka) into one auditable census. Every record and every hex carries a confidence score, and every district carries an estimate of how many chargers are still missing.
  2. Measure honestly. Count only supply that is usable by the vehicles that exist (working probability, connector compatibility, deliverable power, access hours), not raw plugs.
  3. Separate need from viability. Show where chargers are needed and where no private operator will build them without support. That is where public money adds most.
  4. Make every decision explainable. Each recommendation comes with a Decision Card: data lineage, confidence, rupees per EV served, and what would change the answer.

Our four headline differentiators, all built for the Grand Finale: Effective Supply, the Trust Layer (confidence, reconciliation and undercount estimation), the Market-vs-Mandate viability split with a PM E-DRIVE Allocation Advisor, and a policy-versioned norm engine. The demonstration geography is Odisha (data-sparse, high need) in depth, contrasted with Karnataka (data-rich), on top of a national state-level view.

5.1 Census construction

  1. Ingest each source into an immutable raw snapshot with a snapshot ID and date.
  2. Normalise to one schema: site ID; all source IDs; lat/lon; visibility class; operator; port ID; connector; AC/DC; rated kW; supported vehicle classes; hours; status; last-verified date; confidence; flags.
  3. Block candidate duplicates by geohash-7 cell (~150 m) and its eight neighbours, so duplicates that straddle a cell edge are not missed.
  4. Match candidate pairs with a learned classifier (§8) on distance, operator, connector set, power and name similarity. Names only break ties.
  5. Cluster matched records (union-find) into sites and keep the most complete attributes.
  6. Score confidence, estimate the undercount, and reconcile to official counts with a published bridge.

Visibility classes. P1 public 24×7; P2 public, restricted hours; S semi-public (mall, hotel, customers only); C captive company/OEM; F fleet depot; H home/housing society. P1 and P2 count fully as public supply; S counts partially; C, F and H are kept as context and to net out demand (an area full of captive depots needs less public supply).

5.2 Confidence score (false positives)

Confidence = [1 − Π(1 − r_s) over agreeing source groups] × exp(−age / τ) × attribute completeness

r_s is a reliability prior per source (e.g. 0.5 crowd-sourced, 0.8 operator feed, 0.95 field-verified) and τ is a decay constant (12 months). Sources that copy each other (OCM and OSM often share records) are grouped and counted once, so copied records cannot inflate confidence. Priors are flagged assumptions, tunable and shown in the Method Card.

5.3 Undercount estimate (false negatives)

Confidence only rates the chargers we have found. The larger risk is the ones we have not found. For each district we estimate total chargers by capture–recapture across independent source groups (Lincoln–Petersen, or Chao's estimator when there are more than two groups):

N̂ = (n₁ × n₂) / m n₁, n₂ = chargers found by each source group; m = chargers found by both

The estimate is checked against the official count where one exists. The map shows a "what we don't know" layer: districts where the estimated missing share is high, so gaps there are reported as ranges and pushed up the verification queue.

5.4 Reconciliation waterfall and verification queue

A waterfall chart bridges official counts step by step (OMC-only stations → all public stations → points vs stations → captive and semi-public → date differences), turning a disagreement into an auditable explanation. A verification queue ranks sites and districts by uncertainty × decision impact, so a state agency verifies the 200 records that matter most rather than 20,000. Results feed back through a Beta-distribution update of each site's working probability.

7. Analytical methodology

7.1 Effective Supply (ES)

We replace "number of chargers" with Effective Supply: usable kW for a given vehicle segment at a given location.

ES(seg, hex) = Σ over ports p in hex [ min(kW_p, kW_accept(seg)) × P_work(p) × compat(seg, connector_p) × hours_p × access_p ]
  • min(kW_p, kW_accept): deliverable power. A 7.4 kW-limited car on a 22 kW AC point uses a third of it.
  • P_work: working probability, starting from a prior by operator and source recency and updated by verification.
  • compat: the matrix in §6. hours: share of the day the port is open. access: 1 for public, less than 1 or 0 for semi-public or captive.

Demo moment: toggle "raw count" to "Effective Supply" and watch a district turn from green to red.

7.2 EV stock

Stock by segment = cumulative VAHAN registrations minus segment-specific attrition, reconciled to state transport figures, plus an explicit uplift range for unregistered e-rickshaws. Registration RTO is not where a vehicle is used, so hex-level demand is labelled modelled and carries an uncertainty band (§8).

7.3 Public-charging demand (home-charging adjusted)

Energy(seg, hex) = stock × km/day × Wh/km Public share(seg, hex) = 1 − (home- or depot-feasible share × home-charging preference)

Home feasibility comes from dwelling type, own parking, apartment share and fleet use (fleets use depots; cars parked at apartments often cannot plug in at home). Proxies: Census/NSS housing tables, building footprints and density indicators, calibrated against any operator session data obtained. This replaces the most arbitrary input in our pilot with something derived from data, and it explains why dense cities need more public chargers per EV than small towns.

7.4 Forecast

  • A diffusion model (logistic/Bass) fitted to monthly VAHAN series per state and segment, pooled hierarchically so short state series borrow strength from the national curve.
  • Segment-specific saturation levels, not one cap. Reference points are NITI Aayog's 2030 aspirations (about 80% of 2W/3W sales, 70% of commercial cars, 30% of private cars, 40% of buses). Note that e-rickshaws are already the majority of L3 three-wheelers, so a single 30% ceiling would badly under-forecast 3W.
  • Low / Base / High scenarios plus policy-shock cases (subsidy withdrawal or extension). The High scenario is labelled an upper bound where it rests on draft policy.
  • Outputs are shown as prediction bands, not just three lines.

7.5 Need sizing

  1. Peak demand: daily public energy × peak-hour share (8–12%).
  2. Ports by queueing: an Erlang-C model sizes ports so the probability of waiting stays below a target (e.g. 10%).
  3. Split by class: light/AC ports for 2W, 3W and slow 4W; DC ports for fast 4W and commercial; depot energy for buses.

7.6 Corridor model

DC need on a corridor = max(norm-spacing sites, flow-based sites), where flow-based need = through-traffic × EV share × share needing a charge × dwell time. We use published NH traffic counts where available, otherwise a clearly flagged gravity-model proxy. Existing DC sites within 1 km of the corridor are netted off before any new site is proposed. A Highway Range-Safety Index simulates a typical e-car (real range ~250 km, charging 20–80%) along each NH segment and shows where it gets stranded and the single next site that most improves the corridor (demo: Bhubaneswar → Kolkata on NH-16).

7.7 Gap Index and norm engine

  • Gap = need − Effective Supply, by segment and charger class, never raw count.
  • The Gap Index combines three lenses: norm compliance, demand-to-supply, and drive-time access (OSRM isochrones, % population within 5 / 10 / 20 minutes of a working fast charger). It uses fixed reference scales anchored to norms and benchmarks, not min-max across the sample, so a district can be compared over time and with states added later.
  • Policy-versioned norm engine: norms are stored as data (version, effective date, urban grid, highway spacing, heavy-duty spacing). The active version is the MoP 2024 guidelines (one station per 1 km × 1 km urban grid, every 20 km along highways, every 100 km for long-range and heavy-duty vehicles). The earlier 3 km × 3 km / 25 km version and state-specific norms can be switched in, and compliance is recomputed instantly. The version in use is shown on every screen.

7.8 Viability: Market, Support or Mandate

Every hex and corridor segment with a gap gets one label:

  • Market: expected utilisation above break-even. Private operators will build; no subsidy needed.
  • Support: need exists but expected utilisation is below break-even. The support needed is computed site by site as the viability gap (§10.4), not a flat percentage.
  • Mandate: coverage required by norms or equity even though utilisation will stay very low. These sites need capital and operating support, or a mandate on a public operator such as an OMC.

7.9 Siting and the Allocation Advisor

A maximal covering location model with a budget constraint selects sites from candidates (OMC outlets, malls, parking, government and transit land), with a grid-distance penalty. The PM E-DRIVE Allocation Advisor ranks picks by EVs served per rupee of public money and produces a state-wise allocation within the budget. Subsidy parameters are configurable and default to the scheme's notified rates (including how upstream infrastructure is treated), so the advisor follows the scheme's real design.

Decision Card (example). "Rank 4 because 3,100 uncovered EVs within 5 km; nearest working fast charger 38 km away; substation 1.2 km; label: Support; public money needed ₹X; confidence 0.62. Would drop out of the top 10 if the public share for 4W fell below 15%."

7.10 Validation

  • Rolling-origin backtest of the forecast (target MAPE below 20%).
  • Leave-one-district-out validation of the demand disaggregation (§8).
  • Hold-out siting test: hide 30% of existing chargers and measure how many the model recovers (a sanity check, not an accuracy claim).
  • Tornado-chart sensitivity on every assumed parameter, shown in the dashboard.
  • The pilot's worked Baleshwar example is kept as a regression test.

10.3 Unit economics of a public 60 kW DC charger

Coverage norms, not energy demand, drive many DC sites, and those sites will run at low utilisation. So more chargers are needed for coverage than energy alone justifies, and fewer of them are commercially viable. Support is sized to each site's gap; Mandate sites need capital plus operating support (viability-gap funding) or a mandate on a public operator.

Funding and sustainability. (a) A government-funded public good under MHI/BEE, possibly with a small monitoring-and-evaluation share of scheme disbursement; (b) state-level analytics subscriptions; (c) optional paid site-selection and benchmarking reports for CPOs, OEMs and DISCOMs, strictly separated so no commercial user gets privileged access to government data; (d) academic and open-source maintenance through ICAT-linked institutions. 12. Scalability roadmap (5 years)

The roadmap is data-driven in two ways: each stage is gated by metrics the platform computes, and the order of expansion is chosen by a score the platform produces.

StageTimingScopeGate (measured by the platform)
S0 PrototypeDec 2026Odisha in depth, Karnataka for contrast, national state-level view; all deliverables in demo formCensus ≥ 60% of Odisha's official count; VAHAN gap explained; forecast backtest MAPE < 20%
S1 Sponsor pilot2027 (Y1)3–5 states; MoU with MHI/BEE for official lists and a bulk VAHAN feed; first Allocation NoteCensus completeness ≥ 70%; > 50% of supply with confidence ≥ 0.7; at least one state nodal agency uses the Allocation Note
S2 Multi-state2028 (Y2)10–15 states, top ~50 cities, major NH corridors, e-bus depots; CPO/OEM data contract live; PM E-DRIVE tracker before the scheme ends in March 2028Census ≥ 80%; ≥ 10 CPO/OEM feeds; ≥ 70% of top-impact records verified; forecast error stable
S3 National2029 (Y3)All 36 states/UTs; monthly refresh; Gati Shakti layers; DISCOM load moduleCensus ≥ 85%; data freshness ≤ 35 days; ≥ 20 states using outputs
S4 Live and extended2030–31 (Y4–5)Live OCPI-style feeds; heavy-duty and e-truck corridors; megawatt-class charging; swap parityLive uptime for ≥ 40% of fast chargers; norm compliance reported quarterly

Expansion order chosen by the model:

Rollout priority(state) = 0.4 × Need (gap × EV growth) + 0.3 × Data readiness + 0.3 × Institutional readiness

Weights are tunable and shown. Each wave mixes a high-need, data-sparse state (Odisha-like) with a data-rich one (Karnataka-like), so the platform is tested at both ends.

Interoperability. All outputs are published as open-standard layers (GeoJSON, OGC services) ready to overlay in PM Gati Shakti and state GIS platforms. A short OCPI-lite data contract lets CPOs and OEM networks submit data in return for benchmark reports and subsidy-eligibility evidence. Charger depots, swap stations and plug-in chargers share one energy-access schema with kWh-equivalent capacity.

14. Assumptions, limitations and disclosures

  • Pilot numbers (Odisha and Karnataka) come from our Round 1 pilot run on VAHAN and open charger data; the Odisha official figure of 606 stations is unverified.
  • Costs, tariffs and subsidy intensities in Section 10 are illustrative planning ranges, to be replaced with tender, quotation and SERC tariff data. The Market/Support/Mandate shares in 10.5 are illustrative; the model estimates them site by site.

  • Hex-level demand is modelled, not observed; it carries uncertainty bands and is validated leave-one-district-out.
